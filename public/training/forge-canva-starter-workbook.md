# Make the message useful
## A Forge Canva starter workbook for instructors

Original Forge practice material · Version 1, 3 November 2026

Somebody can look at a beautiful page and still not know what to do. Our work is to close that gap. You are learning to make a message clear, and to help a child make a message of their own. You do not need to become a professional designer in two weeks.

Use this with ElyTech Days 8–9. Allow two three-hour sessions, including practice, a reader test, teach-back and short breaks. A paper version is a valid starting point when a device, power or account is unavailable. Use Canva's current official tutorials for its changing interface. This is an independent Forge workbook, not an official Canva publication or certification.

### 1. Start before the editor

Write three sentences:

- My reader is …
- The one thing they need to understand is …
- After reading, they should be able to …

Practice brief: a fictional school library wants learners to return borrowed books on Friday. The notice must be readable on a phone and on a black-and-white A4 printout. Do not invent real school information or use children's names or photographs.

Sketch two layouts on paper. Circle the one where a reader can find the action and date most easily. Keep the other: it is evidence of your thinking, not wasted work.

### 2. Build a page with a clear first step

In a school-approved or personal adult practice account, start a blank design of the intended size, or choose a free template and remove unnecessary content. Account arrangements for children must be handled by the school; do not ask a child to create an unsuitable account. Canva for Education eligibility is separate from joining Forge.

Add your own short heading, one supporting sentence and a clear action/date. Use text boxes you can explain. Choose no more than two typefaces for this exercise. Make the important information visibly different through size, weight or position. A heading that competes with five other headings is not helping the reader.

Practice: make the words “Return your library book” the first thing a reader notices. Make “Friday” easy to find next. Add a short line explaining where to return it. Use invented details and label the design a practice example.

### 3. Four ideas that matter more than the template

**Hierarchy:** what should be noticed first, second and third?

**Alignment:** do related edges line up, or does the reader have to keep searching?

**Contrast:** can the words be read clearly against their background? Check small text especially. Do not use colour as the only way to communicate a difference.

**Space:** is there room around each idea? Empty space is part of the design.

Small game: create a deliberately confusing version of your own notice. Hide the date, compete with the heading, misalign related items. Ask an adult partner to identify three problems. Repair one problem at a time and explain the effect. Do not make the exercise a competition about who has good taste.

### 4. Images need a job

A picture should explain, orient or support the message. If removing it makes the notice clearer, removal may be the right choice. Use your own original drawing, a simple shape or an asset whose terms permit the intended use. A watermark or Pro marker is a reason to choose a permitted alternative, not something to erase.

A raster image is made from pixels: tiny picture elements. Enlarging a small raster image can make its limitations visible. Some graphics use vector shapes and can scale differently. For this first lesson, the practical question is simple: is this image clear enough at the size we will actually use?

Practice: compare a useful book symbol with three decorative images. Keep only what serves the reader. Write one sentence explaining the decision.

### 5. Make instructions somebody can follow

Create a second page: a keyboard practice card.

Goal: the learner uses F and J to find a starting position, then attempts a short set independently.

Use three short numbered actions, one worked example and a space for reflection. For example, the reflection can ask which key pair the learner wants to practise again. Do not require a child to provide a name, private account or photograph.

Basic 1–2 adaptation: use fewer words, a clear demonstration and a paper keyboard. Support letter recognition and movement patiently.

Basic 3–6 adaptation: invite the learner to arrange their own practice card, explain why one step comes first, then test it with a partner. Readiness matters more than assuming every child of the same age needs identical help.

### 6. Test with a reader

Show your page at phone size. Print or preview it in greyscale. Check that important text remains readable when zoomed and that a number or label supports any colour cue.

Give an adult partner one task: “Tell me when and where to return the book”, or “Use this card to begin the practice.” Remain quiet while they try. Write down where the page failed to explain itself.

Ask:

- What did you notice first?
- Which instruction made you pause?
- What did you think would happen next?

Avoid asking only whether they like the colours. Revise at least one thing based on what they did.

### 7. Export and share with care

Use the current editor's download options to export a suitable PDF for your print task or PNG for a simple image task. Export options and paid features may change; choose an available free option. Open the exported file and inspect it rather than assuming it matches the editor.

Before sharing, check names, dates, spelling, image rights and permissions. An editing link is different from a view-only link. For a practice portfolio, share the finished export or the least-permissive suitable link. Keep learner information out of public examples. Do not claim that every PDF export automatically meets accessibility requirements; check the actual output and provide a plain-text explanation where needed.

### 8. Teach it, then keep the evidence

Rehearse a five-minute lesson. Demonstrate one change, let a partner try a different example, then ask them to explain their choice. Help them think; do not finish the design for them.

Keep:

1. Your first sketch and brief.
2. The original learning card and its exported version.
3. One reader observation.
4. One revision and the reason for it.
5. A short teaching plan with a no-device alternative.

Reflection: what can your reader now do that the earlier page made difficult? What could you help a child discover without taking the mouse from them?
